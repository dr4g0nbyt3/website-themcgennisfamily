A Note from Stephanie

A Friday our family looks forward to.

Friday used to be the night we were too tired to cook. Now it's the one we plan around. Welcome to the rhythm we've found.

A few years into raising boys and running businesses, our weeknights had quietly turned into a blur of takeout and "we'll figure it out tomorrow." It worked, but it didn't feel like us. We grew up around grandmothers who could feed a houseful with whatever the pantry held — and we were drifting away from that.

From Scratch Fridays started as a small commitment: one night a week, the meal comes from the kitchen — not a bag, not a box. It didn't have to be fancy. It just had to be ours. Six months in, our boys were asking what we were making before they even said hello on Friday afternoons.

This Starter Pack is everything we wish we'd had when we began. Five recipes that stretch a single afternoon into a real Friday family table, a meal plan you can pin to the fridge, and the pantry checklist that keeps us out of the grocery store on Friday mornings.

Print it, mark it up, lose it under a bag of flour. That's what cookbooks are for.

With love from Jefferson City,
Stephanie Kay McGennis

The Friday Habit

Three rules that make it stick.

The reason a Friday family meal habit fails is almost never the recipe — it's the friction around it. These three small rules removed the friction in our house. They might in yours.

Rule 1 · Decide on Sunday

Friday's recipe is chosen by Sunday night. Not "we'll see what looks good." A specific recipe, picked in advance, kills the Friday-evening "what are we making" spiral that ends in pizza.

Rule 2 · Shop Wednesday

Whatever the recipe needs that isn't pantry-staple, it gets bought by Wednesday. Friday should never be a grocery-store day. Mid-week shopping kept us from arriving home Friday at 5 p.m. realizing we needed buttermilk.

Rule 3 · Start at 4 p.m.

The kitchen opens early. Slow recipes — stews, roasts, breads — work because they're forgiving. Starting at 4 means dinner is ready when everyone is hungry, not an hour after.

Rule 4 · Bonus · Hand someone a job

The boys have learned to peel, stir, knead, set the table. Cooking with kids is messier and slower — and exactly why we do it. The recipes in this pack all have a "kid task" suggestion built in.

"The recipe matters less than the rhythm. Get the rhythm, and the recipes write themselves."
— Stephanie

Recipe One · The Anchor

Sunday Chicken Pot Pie

The recipe we make when the week was long. Buttery crust, real cream, and a filling that turns leftover roast chicken into something that justifies its own afternoon.

Serves
6
Prep
25 min
Cook
45 min
Total
1 hr 10

Ingredients

  • 3 cups cooked chicken, shredded (rotisserie works)
  • 4 tbsp unsalted butter
  • 1 medium yellow onion, diced
  • 2 carrots, peeled and diced
  • 2 celery ribs, diced
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1/3 cup all-purpose flour
  • 2 cups chicken stock
  • 3/4 cup whole milk or half-and-half
  • 1 cup frozen peas
  • 1 tsp fresh thyme (or 1/2 tsp dried)
  • Salt & black pepper, to taste
  • 1 sheet store-bought puff pastry, thawed (or homemade pie crust)
  • 1 egg, beaten (for wash)

Method

  1. Heat oven to 400°F. Melt butter in a large skillet over medium heat.
  2. Add onion, carrots, and celery. Cook 6–8 minutes until soft. Add garlic and cook 1 minute more.
  3. Sprinkle flour over the vegetables and stir for 90 seconds — it should look like a paste.
  4. Whisk in stock and milk. Bring to a simmer; the sauce will thicken in 3–4 minutes.
  5. Stir in chicken, peas, and thyme. Season generously — pot pie filling should taste a touch over-salted; the pastry dilutes it.
  6. Transfer filling to a 9-inch pie dish. Lay puff pastry over the top, trim the edges, and brush with beaten egg.
  7. Cut three slits in the top to vent. Bake 35–40 minutes, until pastry is deep golden and the filling bubbles at the edges.
  8. Rest 10 minutes before serving — this matters; the filling needs to set.
Kid Task Brushing on the egg wash. Slow, satisfying, and impossible to mess up. Older kids can also handle the pastry-trimming with kitchen shears.

Recipe Two · The Slow One

Sunday-Night Beef Stew

A Dutch-oven stew that braises while you do anything else. The forgiving recipe — start it at 4, eat at 7, and the longer it sits, the better it gets.

Serves
6–8
Prep
20 min
Cook
2 hr 30
Total
2 hr 50

Ingredients

  • 2 lbs beef chuck, cut into 1.5-inch cubes
  • 3 tbsp all-purpose flour
  • 2 tbsp olive oil
  • 1 large yellow onion, diced
  • 4 garlic cloves, smashed
  • 2 tbsp tomato paste
  • 1 cup dry red wine (or beef stock)
  • 4 cups beef stock
  • 2 bay leaves, 4 sprigs fresh thyme
  • 4 carrots, peeled and chunked
  • 3 large yukon gold potatoes, cubed
  • 8 oz cremini mushrooms, halved
  • 1 tbsp Worcestershire sauce
  • Salt & black pepper

Method

  1. Heat oven to 325°F. Pat beef dry, season heavily with salt and pepper, and toss in flour.
  2. Heat oil in a Dutch oven over medium-high. Brown beef in two batches — 3–4 minutes per side. Don't crowd the pan; you need real color.
  3. Remove beef. Reduce heat, add onion, cook 5 minutes. Add garlic and tomato paste, cook 90 seconds.
  4. Pour in wine, scraping the bottom. Reduce by half (about 5 minutes).
  5. Return beef. Add stock, bay leaves, thyme, Worcestershire. Bring to a simmer.
  6. Cover and transfer to oven. Braise 90 minutes.
  7. Add carrots, potatoes, and mushrooms. Re-cover and continue braising 45–60 minutes, until vegetables are fork-tender.
  8. Taste and adjust salt. Discard bay leaves and thyme stems before serving with crusty bread.
Make-Ahead This stew is genuinely better the next day. Make it Friday morning if you can — the flavors deepen overnight and reheating is just a gentle simmer.

Recipe Three · The Showstopper

No-Knead Family Bread

A bakery-quality loaf with one stretch, one fold, and almost no skill required. The recipe that taught our boys you can make bread without a mixer, a stand, or any prior expertise.

Yield
1 loaf
Active
15 min
Rest
12–18 hr
Bake
45 min

Ingredients

  • 3 cups bread flour (or all-purpose), plus more for dusting
  • 1 1/2 tsp fine sea salt
  • 1/2 tsp instant yeast
  • 1 1/3 cups cool water (around 70°F)
  • Cornmeal or extra flour, for the bottom

You'll Also Need

  • A large mixing bowl with a lid or plastic wrap
  • A 5–6 quart Dutch oven with a lid
  • Parchment paper

Method

  1. Whisk flour, salt, and yeast in a large bowl. Add water and stir with a wooden spoon until a shaggy, sticky dough forms — about a minute. There will be no kneading.
  2. Cover bowl tightly. Let rise at room temperature 12–18 hours, until the surface is bubbly and the dough has nearly tripled. Overnight is ideal.
  3. Generously flour your hands. Turn the dough out onto a floured surface and shape into a rough ball with two folds — fold the dough over itself, rotate 90°, fold again.
  4. Place dough seam-side-down on a parchment square. Cover with a tea towel; rest 90 minutes.
  5. 30 minutes before baking, place the Dutch oven (with lid) in the oven and heat to 475°F.
  6. Carefully lift the parchment with the dough into the screaming-hot Dutch oven. Score the top with a sharp knife.
  7. Cover, bake 30 minutes. Remove the lid and bake 12–15 more minutes, until the crust is deep mahogany.
  8. Cool on a rack at least 30 minutes before slicing — patience here keeps the crumb from being gummy.
Kid Task Scoring the dough with a knife (with help). It's the only step that feels like art, and they'll remember it.

Recipe Four · The Crowd-Feeder

Three-Bean Family Chili

The recipe we make when there are extra people. Doubles or triples without complaint, freezes well, and gets better every day in the fridge.

Serves
8
Prep
15 min
Cook
1 hr 15
Total
1 hr 30

Ingredients

  • 2 lbs ground beef (85/15)
  • 1 large yellow onion, diced
  • 1 green bell pepper, diced
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 3 tbsp chili powder
  • 2 tsp ground cumin
  • 1 tsp smoked paprika
  • 1 tsp dried oregano
  • 1/2 tsp cayenne (or to taste)
  • 2 tbsp tomato paste
  • 28 oz can crushed tomatoes
  • 15 oz can kidney beans, drained
  • 15 oz can black beans, drained
  • 15 oz can pinto beans, drained
  • 2 cups beef stock
  • 1 tbsp cocoa powder (trust us)
  • Salt & pepper to taste

Method

  1. Heat a large heavy-bottomed pot over medium-high. Brown the beef in two batches, breaking it into small pieces, until well browned. Drain off most of the fat.
  2. Reduce heat to medium. Add onion and bell pepper to the beef; cook 6 minutes. Add garlic and cook 90 seconds.
  3. Add chili powder, cumin, paprika, oregano, and cayenne. Stir for 30 seconds — you want the spices to bloom in the warm fat.
  4. Stir in tomato paste; cook 1 minute until it darkens.
  5. Add crushed tomatoes, all the beans, beef stock, and cocoa powder. Stir well, taste, and season with salt and pepper.
  6. Bring to a simmer, then reduce heat to low. Cook uncovered, stirring occasionally, for 45–60 minutes — the chili should reduce and thicken.
  7. Taste again before serving — you'll usually want another big pinch of salt at the end. Serve with cornbread, sharp cheese, sour cream, and chopped onion.
The Cocoa Trick A single tablespoon of unsweetened cocoa adds depth without a single hint of "chocolate." This is the single biggest secret-weapon move in any chili — every grandmother in our family quietly does this.

Recipe Five · The Sweet Finish

Brown-Butter Apple Crisp

The Friday dessert that tastes like Sunday. Browning the butter for the topping is the single move that turns a fine apple crisp into one your kids will request the next week.

Serves
8
Prep
20 min
Bake
40 min
Total
1 hour

Filling

  • 6 large baking apples (Granny Smith / Honeycrisp mix)
  • 1/3 cup light brown sugar
  • 2 tbsp all-purpose flour
  • 1 tbsp lemon juice
  • 1 1/2 tsp cinnamon
  • 1/4 tsp nutmeg, pinch of salt

Brown-Butter Topping

  • 3/4 cup unsalted butter (1.5 sticks)
  • 1 cup old-fashioned rolled oats
  • 3/4 cup all-purpose flour
  • 2/3 cup light brown sugar
  • 1/2 tsp cinnamon
  • 1/2 tsp salt

Method

  1. Heat oven to 350°F. Butter a 9×9 baking dish.
  2. Brown the butter: melt in a light-colored saucepan over medium. Swirl frequently. After foaming subsides (4–5 min), the butter will smell nutty and golden flecks will form. Pour immediately into a heat-proof bowl. Cool 10 minutes.
  3. Peel, core, and slice apples 1/4-inch thick. Toss with brown sugar, flour, lemon juice, cinnamon, nutmeg, and salt. Pile into the baking dish.
  4. For topping: stir oats, flour, sugar, cinnamon, and salt into the cooled brown butter. Mix until clumps form.
  5. Scatter topping evenly over apples — don't pat it down; you want a craggy top.
  6. Bake 35–45 minutes, until the topping is deep golden and apples are bubbling thickly at the edges.
  7. Rest at least 15 minutes before serving. Vanilla ice cream isn't optional.
Kid Task Mixing the topping. They get to watch butter foam, smell it transform, and feel the topping clump under their hands.

An Interlude

A meal isn't just a meal.

The food is the excuse. The conversation is the meal.

Six months into our Friday habit, we noticed something unexpected. The kids weren't just eating better. They were talking more. The longer prep time, the open kitchen, the unhurried pace — it all created space for the kind of conversation that doesn't happen in a takeout box on the couch.

We started a tradition we call "The Friday Round." After we sit down, we go around the table — one good thing from the week, one hard thing from the week. The boys started small ("I lost a pencil") and grew into real reflection ("a kid was mean to my friend and I didn't know what to do").

The recipes are the structure. The conversation is what fills it.

"The dinner table is the longest schoolroom in our family. The lessons there outlast anything we'd plan."
— Caleb

The McGennis Family 12 / 14 · The Table

A Bonus Page

Build a Friday family table.

The recipes work without this. But the recipes work better with this.

1 · Set the table for guests, even if it's just family

Cloth napkins. The good plates. The candles you only light "for company." You are the company. Once a week is permission to make Friday feel like the weekend.

2 · One question, every Friday

We rotate three: What was a good thing this week? What was a hard thing? What's something you learned? Three minutes of structured conversation does more for a family than three hours of unstructured screen time. Children rise to the level of the questions you ask them.

3 · No phones at the table — including yours

Especially yours. The single fastest way to teach a child that their words don't matter is to glance at a phone while they speak. Charge it on the counter for an hour. The world will manage.

4 · End with thanks, not screens

The dishes are part of the meal. Hand someone a towel. Let the conversation wind down naturally instead of jumping to a movie. Some of our boys' best stories have come during the dishwashing afterglow.

"We're not trying to teach our boys to cook. We're trying to teach them what a home is for."
— Stephanie & Caleb
